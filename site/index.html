AI as Area Chair: What Science Would AI Send to ICML?

AI agents are starting to be used across the research pipeline, and as more of that pipeline gets automated, it is increasingly important to understand what kind of research they favor. To better understand this, as a pilot study, we had AI agents rank all 6,617 papers accepted to ICML 2026 and mimic the area chair (AC)'s role, and we compared that ranking with what the conference honored.

These are the first pages of the five papers that the AI models ranked highest among all 6,617 papers accepted to ICML 2026.

AI agents are starting to be utilized across the research pipeline, from searching for algorithms (e.g., AlphaEvolve) to drafting full papers (e.g., The AI Scientist), and a growing set of autoresearch systems now propose hypotheses and filter ideas before a person reviews them. As we automate parts of, or eventually the entire, end-to-end research pipeline with such systems, we need to understand how their research taste differs from that of humans. This is important because we do not want human research taste to converge one-sidedly on the model's, or the field to lose the diversity it has now.

To examine this, we ran a pilot study on ICML 2026. Research taste is difficult to either measure directly or compare against, because in most cases it is unusual to say that one piece of research is better than another. However, conferences are one of the few settings where this comparison is made explicitly, since papers are scored, compared, and then designated as orals, spotlights, or awards. This gives us a setting where research taste has to become a concrete decision, and it also gives us a human judgment that we can compare against. Specifically, similar to the role of an area chair, who reads a set of papers, weighs them against one another, and recommends which of them should be put forward, we asked a panel of AI models to rank all 6,617 accepted papers into a single ordering. We reproduced only this comparative part of the role, since the models saw no rebuttals or reviewer discussion, and they made no accept or reject decisions.

01

Methodology: Our pipeline for AI to rank the 6,617 papers

The panel combined several models since we used cheaper models to narrow the corpus and stronger models on the later rounds.

  • Stage 01 6,617 papers

    Blind and route. Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite reads the identity-redacted first nine pages of every paper and labels its contribution type, so that later stages compare papers of a similar kind. No papers are removed here.

  • Stage 02 6,617 → 1,442

    Cheap recall ensemble. Four inexpensive judges score every paper under two randomized contexts each, which gives eight judgments per paper. This stage is tuned for recall rather than precision, since a paper cut here can never be recovered later.

  • Stage 03 1,442 → 250

    Strong semifinal. GPT-5.6 Terra and Claude Sonnet 5 rank the shortlist in listwise batches, with two partitions per judge, and we average their normalized ranks with equal weight. The finalist selection is deliberately conservative, and it keeps per-class leaders and papers the judges disagreed on.

  • Stage 04 250 → 172

    Frontier PDF panel. GPT-5.6 Sol, Claude Fable 5, and Gemini 3.1 Pro each read every finalist once and write a structured judgment card, which gives 750 cards in total. The later stages reuse these cards rather than regenerating them.

  • Stage 05 172 → 60

    Swiss pool. The cards feed both an equal-weight three-judge ensemble and a GPT-5.6 Sol synthesis, and the union of their top 150 forms the pool. GPT-5.6 Sol then compares papers pairwise over 10 Swiss rounds, or 860 pairs.

  • Stage 06 60 papers

    All-pairs playoff. Every one of the 60 papers is compared against every other, giving a complete graph of 1,770 pairs. This stage orders papers rather than removing them, and it produces the final ranking.

We asked all of these models to identify the papers that were most technically sound, most consequential for machine learning, and most important for scientific progress. We wrote this rubric to be broad, and to stay close to the criteria that conferences already publish for reviewers. The models saw the papers, but not the authors, reviews, reviewer scores, area-chair comments, decisions, presentation tiers, or awards.

Human and AI research tastes are different

Finding 01

Ranking correlation between human and AI is slightly more than random chance

ICML leaves two records of what it thought of each accepted paper. The first is how prominently the paper was presented: a small number were chosen as orals, a larger group as spotlights, and the rest as posters. The second is the average score the paper's reviewers gave it. We compared the AI ranking against both using Kendall's rank correlation, which takes every possible pair of papers and asks whether the AI and the conference put that pair in the same order, then reports the share of pairs they agree on minus the share they disagree on, counting only pairs the conference itself ranked differently. A score of 0 would mean the two orderings carry no information about each other, and 1 would mean they are identical. Against both records the AI ranking scores 0.08, which is close enough to zero that it is worth asking whether there is any relationship at all. The two figures that follow show that there is a relationship, and that it is a weak one.

The AI ranking sorts all 6,341 main-track papers from first to last, so its top tenth is the 634 papers it rated highest. The figure above asks how much of each presentation tier reached that group. Had the AI ordered papers without regard to what the conference decided, the members of any tier would be scattered through the ranking at random, and about one in ten of them would end up in the top tenth by chance alone. That is what the dashed line marks. In fact a quarter of the orals reached it, against about one poster in eleven, which makes an oral roughly 2.8 times as likely as a poster to be rated highly by the AI. So there is a relationship. It is a weak one, since three quarters of the papers the conference chose as orals did not reach the AI's top tenth at all.

The figure above asks the same question of the reviewer scores, which are a finer measure than the three presentation tiers. Position in the AI ranking is shown as a percentile, where 100 is the top of the ranking and 0 is the bottom, so a higher number means the AI rated the paper more highly. Papers that reviewers scored 3.25 sit on average at the 44th percentile, slightly below the middle of the AI ranking, and papers that reviewers scored 5.25 sit at the 61st, slightly above it. The line rises from left to right, so better-reviewed papers do tend to rank higher with the AI. It rises gently. The entire range of human opinion, from the lowest reviewer scores to the highest, moves a paper less than 18 percentile points on average.

Finding 02

The disagreement has a consistent shape across contribution types

Weak agreement on its own does not tell us what each side prefers, because a correlation can be low for many reasons. To compare the two directly we need two sets of the same size, so we take the 536 papers the conference chose as orals or spotlights and the 536 papers the AI ranked highest, and compare what kinds of work each set contains. The two sets pull apart in a consistent direction. The AI holds more core machine-learning algorithm papers, more safety and evaluation work, and more infrastructure. The conference holds more theory, more analysis and position papers, and far more application methods. Both sides move away from application papers relative to the corpus, and the AI simply goes much further.

Finding 03

Research taste is more similar between two AI models than between AI and humans

Nemotron 3 Ultra, Gemini 3.5 Flash Lite, GPT-5.6 Luna, and Grok 4.3 were built by separate organizations on separate data, and they were asked to rank the same 6,341 papers. They agree with each other at a rank correlation of 0.62 on average, and the weakest pair of them still reaches 0.57. Each of them agrees with the human reviewer score at between 0.07 and 0.10, so the poorest agreement between any two models is far above the best agreement between any model and the reviewers.

There is a wrinkle in that comparison. The models were not scoring papers one at a time. Each was handed the same batches of eight and asked to rank a paper against the others in front of it, so two models compared across the whole corpus get some credit for a batch layout they happened to share. We can take that back out by staying inside a single batch, where every model read the same eight papers and the reviewer score can be read off those same eight. Measured that way a model orders a batch the way another company's model would at 0.43, and the way the human reviewers did at 0.07. The gap does not narrow when the context is held identical on both sides. A taste that only one model held would be a quirk of that model and would disappear as soon as someone used a different one, and this one does not behave like that.

04

The most structural difference: AI forecasts where work will lead, humans rewarded novelty

Humans and AI are grading different things. AI asks where a paper is likely to lead and scores it on that forecast. Human reviewers rewarded the paper in front of them, and what they rewarded most was whether it was new. Because the models write their reasoning down on every paper they read, we can read this difference off their own writing rather than inferring it from the ranking.

Finding 01

The criterion the AI leaned on hardest is the one reviewers tracked least

Every paper that reached the frontier panel was scored on six named axes, so we can ask which of them the AI actually followed. Ranking the six by how much each one moved the AI's own ordering puts predicted ML field impact first at 0.80. Ranking the same six by how much each moved the score human reviewers gave puts predicted field impact last at 0.06, low enough that its confidence interval still includes zero. Novelty runs the other way, first for the reviewers at 0.24 and fifth for the AI. Predicted field impact outweighs novelty as a driver of the AI's ordering by 0.21 and falls short of it as a driver of the reviewer score by 0.18, and both gaps survive resampling.

Finding 02

The AI decides field impact by asking whether the result generalizes, and humans did not price that at all

The first pass wrote down its reasoning for all 6,341 main-track papers, not only for the finalists, so its arguments can be counted at the scale of the whole corpus. The objection it reaches for most often is that a result may not hold beyond the setting it was demonstrated in, and that objection tracks its ranking closely. As more of the four models raise it, a paper's average position falls from the 53rd percentile to the 43rd. The same objection tells you nothing about what humans did. It was raised against 22.0% of the papers ICML made orals, 15.1% of the spotlights, and 18.4% of the posters, which is not an ordering at all. Every other argument behaves the same way, moving the AI substantially and the reviewer score barely, whether the argument is a reason to promote a paper or a reason to hold it back.

The same taste, on six named papers

Because the reasoning is recorded for every paper, we can read what the AI said about the papers where it disagreed with humans most. Across the main track, 531 papers that ICML left as posters reached the top tenth of the AI ranking, and 35 papers that ICML chose as orals or spotlights fell into its bottom tenth. The six below are the most extreme cases in each direction, on one shared scale, which is position in the AI ranking of all 6,341 main-track papers.

Every quoted sentence is an argument about how far the work will travel. The three papers the AI lifted are praised for general theory, unusually broad empirical claims, and clear field-level consequences. The three it buried are faulted on the mirror image of those grounds, for combining existing techniques, for limited transferability beyond one benchmark family, and for depending on datasets assembled for the paper itself. On the last of them another of the eight judgments puts it plainly, saying its impact is "primarily in clinical neuroscience, not general ML." Human ACs made that paper an oral.

Both lists are rule-defined rather than chosen by hand. A paper counts as buried if ICML made it an oral, a spotlight, or an award winner and the AI placed it in the bottom 10%, and as elevated if ICML made it a poster and the AI placed it in the top 10%. Each pool is sorted by how far the two judgments diverge, and the table shows the head of each. All four models placed each of these papers on the side shown, and the quoted sentence is the shortest of the eight they wrote.

05

Limitations

We did not run this experiment to replicate human area chairs with AI. We used the role because it gave us a human judgment over the same papers, already written down, to compare against.

  • Not peer review. Models saw paper artifacts, not rebuttals, reviewer discussion, community context, or downstream evidence.
  • Not one human signal but two. Reviewer scores and committee decisions are different targets, and a ranking can move closer to one while moving away from the other. Every human-side number here inherits that ambiguity.
  • Not a stable ranking. The AI is unreliable against itself. Three of the four first-stage models preferred whichever of two papers they happened to read earlier, and correcting for that moves the average paper 230 places and leaves only 23 of the original top 50 in place.
  • Not a ground-truth ranking. Both the AI ranking and the conference outcomes are noisy and contingent. Nothing here says who was right.
  • Not model-independent. Results depend on the selected models, prompts, rubric, context limits, and possible training-data contamination.
  • Not a full acceptance study. This analysis ranks the accepted corpus; it does not reconstruct the complete submission and rejection process.
06

Conclusion and position: in a world full of autonomous research agents, the human's role is to disagree

Every researcher answers one question before any other: what is worth working on? That question has never had a common source. People have answered it separately, out of their own training, circumstances, and luck. Research agents are about to answer it for everybody at once. What a researcher does when they disagree with that answer will matter more than ever.

A field covers ground because its members choose for unrelated reasons. One person works on a problem because of where they trained, another because a collaborator got them interested. A third holds the only dataset, and a fourth was told it would not work and kept going anyway. Most of those directions lead nowhere, and nobody can tell in advance which ones will. That is exactly why the spread matters, since the rare direction that pays off is seldom one anyone could have argued for at the start. Nobody designed this and nobody maintains it.

Model answers do not vary that way. Four models built by four different companies ordered the same 6,341 papers far more like each other than like the humans who reviewed them. It makes little difference which one a researcher asks. Asking for something unusual does not help either, since everyone who asks gets the same unusual answer. The reasons behind everyone's choices stop being independent. Coverage depends on how much the answers differ from each other, and that is not something anyone tracks. A better model may narrow it rather than widen it, since a recommendation people trust is one they override less often.

That leaves one thing a person can supply and a model cannot. Someone has to work on what the ranking buried: a result that will not generalize, a population too small to move any benchmark, a payoff that arrives in fifteen years. Our own ranking pushed the first of those down, and said so in its own words. Disagreeing out loud is cheap. Spending five years on what the model ranked last is the contribution.

None of this follows from our ranking, and we are not claiming it does. We ranked finished papers, which is the end of the pipeline rather than the point where these choices get made. It is why we wanted the ranking anyway. This is a pilot study on one conference at one moment, and it should be run again as both sides change. So we are releasing the full ranking, the intermediate judgments, and the code.
