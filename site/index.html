AI as Area Chair: What Science Would AI Send to ICML?

AI increasingly helps plan, write, and review science. We took that influence to its logical extreme: what if AI alone decided which ICML papers mattered most?

An independent ranking experiment across the ICML 2026 accepted corpus

We asked a panel of AI systems to identify the papers that were most technically sound, most consequential for machine learning, and most important for scientific progress. The models saw the papers. They did not see the authors, reviews, reviewer scores, area-chair comments, decisions, presentation tiers, or awards.

Only after the ranking was frozen did we compare it with ICML's human-led outcomes. The result is neither agreement nor rebellion. AI found a set rich in papers humans also honored, then imposed a distinctly different ordering on it.

The experiment

From 6,617 papers to a 60-paper final

The frontier panel read each finalist once. Its reusable cards fed both an equal-weight three-judge ensemble and Sol synthesis; their top-150 union formed the 172-paper Swiss pool. Only the final 60 received a complete pairwise graph.

AI versus ICML

Agreement without imitation

Human-honored papers were 4.7x more common in AI's top 10 than in the main-track baseline.

Four of the top 10 were human orals or spotlights. Yet only two of ICML's seven award papers reached the 250 finalists, one reached the final 60, and none reached AI's top 10.

Across all main-track papers, AI distinguished oral/spotlight papers from posters with an AUC of 0.602: real agreement, but far from replication.

A different theory of importance

AI preferred general mechanisms over specialized applications

AI's finalist slate overrepresented core algorithms, theory, scientific tools, and safety/evaluation work. Application-specific methods fell from 18.4% of the corpus to 1.6% of the finalists.

This was not a missing-data artifact. Sampled high-human, low-AI papers had all eight expected cheap-model judgments, and the models consistently ranked them low. The divergence is a preference of the rubric and models, not a broken batch.

05

Purpose and limitations

A thought experiment, not a proposal for AI area chairs

We are not advocating that AI replace reviewers or area chairs. We make this counterfactual concrete because AI preferences, for better or worse, already enter science long before any final decision: in brainstorming, research planning, writing, reviewing, and rebuttal. Even without formal authority, models can influence which questions are pursued, which claims appear important, and which weaknesses receive attention.

Why run the experiment?

A reproducible, deliberately extreme thought experiment makes those scientific preferences inspectable. The rankings and intermediate judgments let researchers study what current models reward, overlook, and value differently from a human-led conference.

What can it not establish?

  • Not peer review. Models saw paper artifacts, not rebuttals, reviewer discussion, community context, or downstream evidence.
  • Not a ground-truth ranking. Both AI judgments and conference outcomes reflect noisy, contingent preferences.
  • Not model-independent. Results depend on the selected models, prompts, rubric, context limits, and possible training-data contamination.
  • Not a full acceptance study. This analysis ranks the accepted corpus; it does not reconstruct the complete submission and rejection process.

As conferences and models evolve, repeated snapshots can track whether human and AI preferences become more aligned. If they do, agreement alone will not explain why: are models becoming better scientific judges, are humans delegating more judgment to AI, or are both happening? This release provides a baseline for investigating that distinction.
